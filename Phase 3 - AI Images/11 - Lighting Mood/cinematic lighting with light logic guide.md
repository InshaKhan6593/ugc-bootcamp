## What you're building

Use light logic to stop AI images from looking random or fake. The goal is not to write 'cinematic lighting' and hope the model understands. The goal is to tell the AI where the light comes from, what kind of light it is, what mood it creates, and how it behaves across the whole scene.

![](cinematic%20lighting%20with%20light%20logic%20guide_images/img_p2_2.jpg)

photograph: a woman sitting in a minimalist room with the text "It feels like a render, not a real moment." overlaid on a black background

Vague lighting prompts create render-like images: evenly bright, no clear source, no direction, and no believable shadow logic.

<!-- page 3 -->

## 1. Give the light a real source

Light has to come from somewhere. A window, the sun, a lamp, headlights, a doorway, fire, neon, a practical bulb, or a screen can all motivate the lighting. The source does not need to be visible in the shot, but the viewer should be able to read where it is coming from by looking at the shadows.

* Bad lighting logic: the whole frame is evenly bright with no source or direction.

* Better lighting logic: name the source and direction, then let the shadows prove it.

* Do not force the lamp or window into the middle of the frame if it ruins the composition. Let the source sit off-frame if needed.

photograph: A cinematic portrait of a woman in a desert house with directional lighting, accompanied by a detailed prompt.

![](cinematic%20lighting%20with%20light%20logic%20guide_images/img_p3_2.jpg)

A believable source gives the light direction: one side of the subject is lit, the other side falls into shadow.

### Prompt structure

**Weak**: cinematic lighting, realistic portrait, beautiful room.
**Better**: soft daylight from a large window on the left, light falling across one side of the face and body, opposite side dropping into soft shadow.

## 2. Choose hard light or soft light

Hard light and soft light create completely different feelings. Hard light comes from a small direct source, like bare sun or a sharp lamp. It creates strong shadows with defined edges. Soft light comes from a large or diffused source, like a big window or overcast sky. It creates gentle shadows and smoother transitions.

* Hard light feels dramatic, tense, raw, harsh, or dangerous.

* Soft light feels calm, expensive, intimate, flattering, or controlled.

* Choose the quality of light based on the story, not because one is always better.

## 3. Control the direction of the light

Light direction decides how much shape the subject has. Side light carves out texture and form. Backlight separates the subject from the background and creates rim light. Front light flattens the face, which can be boring for drama but useful for beauty or fashion.

* Side light: best for cinematic shape, texture, and shadow contrast.

* Backlight: useful for silhouettes, separation, rim light, and depth.

* Front light: useful for clean beauty, glossy fashion, and polished skin, but often too flat for dramatic scenes.

* The shadow side matters as much as the lit side. Shadow is what gives the subject shape.

photograph: a man on a film set with a motorcycle and lighting equipment, featuring an AI Video Bootcamp watermark

![](cinematic%20lighting%20with%20light%20logic%20guide_images/img_p5_2.jpg)

Real production lighting uses direction, haze, practical sources, and background separation instead of lighting everything equally.

Prompt structure

`hard side light from the left, deep shadow on the right, background darker than the subject, motivated practical light behind the subject`

## 4. Use colour temperature to set emotion

Colour temperature changes how a shot feels before the viewer even thinks about it. Warm light feels alive, intimate, nostalgic, human, or inviting. Cool light feels distant, lonely, modern, hard, or controlled. Green light can make a location feel uneasy, artificial, sick, or slightly wrong.

* Warm light: golden, orange, alive, intimate, nostalgic.

* Cool light: blue, grey, distant, lonely, modern, controlled.

* Green tint: uneasy, artificial, sick, industrial, off.

* Red light: danger, alarm, pressure, passion, urgency, tension.

## 5. Motivate strong colour choices

Coloured light works best when it has a reason to exist. Red light is powerful, but it should come from something believable: a safelight, alarm, neon sign, brake light, exit sign, police light, or emergency glow. If the source is motivated, it reads as cinema. If not, it can feel like a cheap filter.

![](cinematic%20lighting%20with%20light%20logic%20guide_images/img_p7_2.jpg)

Medium close-up, low eye-level, three-quarter view, subject off-center. The woman from @image1 in a near-dark photographic darkroom, holding a strip of film up toward the red safelight, examining it, her face turned up to the light.

Keep her face and braids exactly as in @image1, natural green eyes. Outfit: brown oversized leather jacket, cream crop top, silver hoops.

Lighting: the only light is a single dim red darkroom safelight, so the room is mostly deep shadow and near-black, with the dim red glow reaching only the parts of her closest to the lamp.

Her face, hands and the film glow red where the light touches, while everything further away falls into dark red-black shadow. No other light source, no neutral or white areas, the lit areas red, the rest swallowed by darkness. Tense and charged.

Red light instantly creates danger and tension when it is motivated by a visible or believable source.

`single red overhead practical light, dark room, red glow falling across the subject, deep surrounding shadows, tense cinematic mood`

## 6. Combine warm and cool for contrast

Warm and cool light work well together because orange and blue sit opposite each other on the colour wheel. Warm skin against a cooler background is one of the classic cinematic looks. It separates the subject from the world and adds depth on top of mood.

* Use warm light on the subject and cooler tones in the background for separation.

* Repeat one accent colour through the frame so the image feels intentional.

* If the subject has orange details, echo orange in a reflection, object, background light, or prop.

The classic orange-and-teal complementary contrast, warm skin against cool shadows, the two opposite colors making each other pop.

Warm subject light against cool window/background tones creates colour contrast and makes the frame feel more composed.

**Prompt structure**

`warm key light on the subject, cool blue-green ambient window light in the background, subtle orange accent repeated in the frame, cinematic colour contrast`

## 7. Make materials react to light

A shot feels physical when every surface reacts to light in its own way. Metal throws sharp highlights. Glass catches light and lets it through. Glossy car paint reflects like a mirror. Stone has a deep smooth sheen. Fabric absorbs light and stays softer. Smoke, mist, water, skin, and polished objects all behave differently.

* For product shots, describe how the light reflects on the product surface.

* For fashion, describe how fabric absorbs or catches light.

* For cars, watches, glass, or cosmetics, describe reflections, highlights, shine, and texture.

* If everything reacts the same way, the image starts to feel like a render.

photograph: a watch on a dark reflective surface with AI Video Bootcamp logo

![](cinematic%20lighting%20with%20light%20logic%20guide_images/img_p9_2.jpg)

Product lighting needs controlled highlights and reflections so metal, glass, and polished surfaces feel real.

**Prompt structure**
`controlled studio product lighting, sharp specular highlights on metal, soft reflection on glass, dark reflective surface, subtle haze behind the product`

## 8. Anchor the light source first

If you are building multiple images from the same scene, the lighting has to stay consistent. Anchor the main source in the first shot, then keep that logic everywhere else. If the wide shot establishes a large window on the left, every closer shot should still feel like the light comes from that same direction.

* Anchor the main light source in the first shot.

* Decide its direction, softness, colour, and contrast.

* Do not let every new shot invent a new lighting setup unless the scene actually changes.

Anchor the light source once: a large window or opening establishes where the daylight comes from.

## 9. Keep close-ups on the same lighting logic

Close-ups need to follow the same light logic as the wide shot. The subject can be framed differently, but the light should still come from the same side, keep the same softness, and preserve the same shadow behaviour.

photograph: close-up of a woman with braids in a desert house interior

![](cinematic%20lighting%20with%20light%20logic%20guide_images/img_p11_2.jpg)

**PROMPT:**

Close-up, eye-level, three-quarter view, subject off-center. Tight on the face of the woman from @image1 in the desert house interior from @image2, no window visible in frame.

She turns her head slightly toward the light.

Keep her face and braids exactly as in @image1, natural green eyes.

Lighting: soft daylight from the large window on the left as the single source, warm tone, lighting the left side of her face and body.

The right side falls into medium soft shadow, the room darker away from the window, direction clearly readable.

Shot on ARRI Alexa LF, 85mm, f1.8. Muted desaturated warm grade, earthy desert tones. Ultra realistic.

Closer shots should keep the same window-light direction, shadow side, and colour logic as the wide shot.

Prompt structure

```
same character, close-up shot, soft daylight from the large window on the
left, warm light on one side of the face, soft shadow on the opposite side,
same room lighting logic
```

## The lighting checklist

Before writing any cinematic lighting prompt, answer these questions. If you cannot answer them, the AI will probably invent random light for you.

* Where is the light coming from?

* Is the light hard or soft?

* Which direction does it hit the subject from?

* What colour temperature or accent colour sets the emotion?

* How do the materials in the scene react to that light?

* If this is a sequence, does the lighting stay consistent from shot to shot?

**The big idea:** cinematic lighting is not a vague style word. It is logic. Source, direction, shape, colour, material, and continuity. Once the light makes sense, the scene starts to feel believable.
