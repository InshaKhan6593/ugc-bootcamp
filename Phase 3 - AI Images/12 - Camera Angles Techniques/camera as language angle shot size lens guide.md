## What you're building

Use camera language to direct AI images and videos instead of letting the model choose random framing. The camera is not just recording the scene. It tells the viewer how to read the subject, how close they should feel, how much information they should notice, and how the space itself should feel.

The camera changes the meaning of the same person and scene by changing position, distance, and lens choice.

## 1. Treat the camera as the viewer's position

The first question is not just whether the camera is up or down. The real question is: where is the viewer standing in the scene? Camera angle controls status, intimacy, tension, power, vulnerability, and point of view.

* Eye level feels honest and equal.

* Low angle makes the subject feel powerful, heroic, or threatening.

* High angle can make the subject feel vulnerable, exposed, or small.

* Dutch angle makes the world feel unstable or wrong.

## 2. Use side, frontal, back, shoulder, and POV angles intentionally

Height is only one part of camera angle. The side you shoot from also changes the relationship. A frontal shot confronts the viewer. A three-quarter angle adds shape and depth. A profile can feel distant. A back shot creates mystery. Over-the-shoulder and POV shots place the viewer inside the scene.

* Frontal shot: direct, symmetrical, iconic, or confrontational.

* Three-quarter angle: natural, cinematic, and dimensional.

* Profile shot: distant, thoughtful, or closed off.

* Over-the-shoulder: useful for conversations, power dynamics, and confrontations.

* POV: the camera becomes the character's eyes.

## 3. Do not assign one fixed meaning to an angle

A low angle usually suggests power, but on a scared child looking up at an adult, it can communicate fear. A high angle often suggests weakness, but it can also show order or control. The angle points the scene in a direction; the character, lighting, and story decide the final meaning.

* Camera angle is direction, not a fixed rule.

* Read the subject and moment before deciding what the angle means.

* Combine camera position with lighting, expression, environment, and action.

## 4. Pick shot size based on what the viewer needs to read

Shot size is not just about far or close. It decides what information the viewer is supposed to read: the world, body, action, reaction, emotion, or one tiny detail.

* Extreme wide: the environment becomes the real subject.

* Wide shot: the viewer reads the person and place together.

* Full shot: posture, outfit, silhouette, and stance matter.

* Medium shot: natural conversation distance, but boring if overused.

* Close-up: emotion, eyes, breath, and tiny changes take over.

## 5. Use contrast between wide and tight shots

A close-up only hits if the viewer has been held at a different distance first. If every shot is tight, close-ups stop feeling special. The jump between a huge wide and one tiny detail is where tension lives.

* Use wide shots to establish scale and environment.

* Use medium shots for action, behaviour, and conversation.

* Use close-ups when emotion or reaction needs to dominate.

* Use extreme close-ups for one critical detail only.

## 6. Understand what lens choice changes

Two shots can have the same subject, angle, and size but feel completely different because of the lens. The lens changes the geometry of the image: how deep the space feels, how close the background appears, and how aggressive or calm the frame feels.

* Wide lenses make space feel deep and immersive.

* Normal lenses feel calm and close to human vision.

* Long lenses compress space and feel more distant or controlled.

* Lens choice should match the emotional job of the frame.

## 7. Separate focal length from camera position

Focal length controls field of view. Camera position controls perspective. A wide lens does not distort a face by itself. Distortion happens when the camera is pushed close to the face. In prompts, describe both the lens and where the camera stands.

* 16-24mm: wide, deep, immersive, useful for interiors, POV, cars, and action.

* 40-50mm: natural, calm, close to human perspective.

* 85mm: narrower, good subject separation and portrait feel.

* 200mm and beyond: compressed, distant, stacked planes.

## 8. Use aperture and subject distance together

Aperture controls how much of the image stays sharp, but subject distance and background distance matter just as much. F1.4 gives a shallow depth of field when the subject has separation from the background. F8 keeps more of the scene sharp.

* Small F-number means a wider opening and blurrier background.

* Large F-number means a smaller opening and more sharpness.

* Move the subject away from the background if you want stronger blur.

* Do not rely on aperture alone; describe distance too.

## 9. Choose camera bodies for their look

Camera names can help communicate a visual character. They are not magic words, but they can guide the model toward a type of image: serious drama, low-light cinema, sharp modern action, or premium commercial surfaces.

* ARRI Alexa: serious drama, soft highlight rolloff, strong all-round cinema look.

* Sony Venice 2: clean night and low-light work.

* RED V-Raptor: sharp, modern, action-friendly digital cinema.

* Hasselblad / medium format: glossy premium product and luxury surface look.

## 10. Use the three-question framework before prompting

Before writing any cinematic prompt, answer three questions: where is the camera, how much do we see, and how does the space feel? Once you answer those, the frame becomes directed instead of random.

* Angle answers: where is the camera?

* Shot size answers: how much do we see?

* Lens answers: how does the space feel?

* Camera body adds: what image character are we aiming for?

## The big idea

Camera direction is one of the fastest ways to make AI images and videos feel intentional. Do not just describe the subject. Tell the model where the camera stands, what the viewer should read, and how the lens should make the space feel.

* Angle creates relationship and power.

* Shot size controls information.

* Lens controls the feeling of space.

* Camera body references help steer the visual character.

* When you control all three, you stop generating random frames and start directing.
