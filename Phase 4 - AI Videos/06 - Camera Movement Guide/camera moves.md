## What you're building

A practical camera-move prompt library for directing AI video with intention. Instead of asking for generic movement, each framework gives the model a clear reason to move: realization, scale, threat, geography, emotion, rhythm, or reveal.

## 30 cinematic camera-move frameworks

**Each entry includes:**

* Core idea and what changes

* Use cases and shot/lens guidance

* Safer version for cleaner AI outputs

* Risks to avoid messy movement

* Prompt template, example, and short add-on line

Use this as a director's reference: choose the camera move first, then adapt the template to your scene, subject, lighting, pacing, and emotional trigger.

## Slow Motivated Push-In

The camera begins to move at the exact moment of realization, decision, threat detection, or internal change.

Shot: Medium, Medium Close-Up, Close-Up · Lens: 50mm, 65mm, 75mm

### What changes

The camera begins to move at the exact moment of realization, decision, threat detection, or internal change.

### Use cases

Realization, decision, threat, emotional shift.

### Safer version

Use a short, straight push-in with a clear trigger and a locked final frame.

### Risks

If there is no trigger, the move becomes generic drift. Too long a push-in can become mushy or slow-motion-like.

### Prompt template

Begin on [Frame A]. After [trigger], start a slow physical dolly in toward [subject]. Keep the move subtle and motivated, with controlled ease-in, gentle momentum build, and a precise ease-out into [Frame B]. Real-time action, no slow motion.

### Example

Locked-off medium shot of a woman in a quiet kitchen. After she notices the knife missing from the counter, begin a slow physical dolly in toward her face. Subtle ease-in, gentle build of momentum, real-time movement, ending in a locked close-up as fear becomes visible in her expression.

Short add-on line: Begin on [Frame A].

## Pull-Out Scale Reveal

The shot begins tight, then expands outward to reveal the true scale of the world, danger, crowd, architecture, or environment.

Shot: Close-Up to Wide, Medium to Extreme Wide · Lens: 24mm, 28mm, 35mm

### What changes

The shot begins tight, then expands outward to reveal the true scale of the world, danger, crowd, architecture, or environment.

### Use cases

Scale, consequence, geography, surprise.

### Safer version

Start with one clear subject and reveal one major layer of information.

### Risks

If the reveal is vague, the move loses purpose. If the environment is underdescribed, the reveal feels empty.

### Prompt template

Begin tight on [subject/detail]. Perform a smooth dolly out or crane out to reveal [environment/scale]. Preserve real perspective change, layered depth, and a readable final wide frame. End with a stable hold on the revealed world.

### Example

Begin on a close-up of a firefighter's soot-covered face inside a dark hallway. Perform a smooth dolly out to reveal that he is standing inside a vast burning hotel atrium filled with smoke, falling debris, and panicked civilians. Maintain real perspective change and end in a readable wide composition that lands on the full scale of the emergency.

Short add-on line: Begin tight on [subject/detail].

## Layered Parallax Push-In

The camera pushes toward the subject through close foreground layers, creating depth, voyeurism, and physical entry into the scene.

Shot: Medium Wide, Medium, Medium Close-Up · Lens: 24mm, 28mm, 35mm

### What changes

The camera pushes toward the subject through close foreground layers, creating depth, voyeurism, and physical entry into the scene.

### Use cases

Depth, immersion, surveillance feeling, physical presence.

| Safer version             | Risks                          |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Use one or two clear      | Too many foreground layers can |
| foreground layers only:   | create clutter. Long lenses    |
| doorframe, glass, fabric, | reduce the effect. Weak        |
| plants, shoulder.         | foreground kills the parallax. |

### Prompt template

Push in toward [subject] through close foreground elements such as [foreground list]. Emphasize layered depth and near-field parallax. The camera should physically travel through space, not digitally zoom. End with a cleaner, closer frame on the subject.

### Example

Push in toward a detective standing in a dim archive room through a blurred foreground of hanging plastic sheets and filing shelves. Emphasize layered depth, strong near-field parallax, and the feeling of physically entering the space. The camera travels through the room and ends in a tighter medium close-up on the detective as he finds the hidden file.

Short add-on line: Push in toward [subject] through close foreground elements such as [foreground list].

### Risks

## Top-Down Graphic Tracking

A strict overhead camera tracks along a route, turning people, vehicles, or objects into graphic shapes inside a system.

Shot: Top-Down Wide or Medium Wide · Lens: 24mm, 28mm, 35mm equivalent overhead

### What changes

A strict overhead camera tracks along a route, turning people, vehicles, or objects into graphic shapes inside a system.

### Use cases

Geography, routes, systems, stylization, tactical clarity.

### Safer version

Keep the route simple and readable. Use strong shapes and clean separation.

### Risks

Messy production design or unclear paths reduce the graphic effect. Too much tilt breaks the top-down logic.

### Prompt template

Use a strict top-down camera. Track above [subject/path/system] as it moves through [space]. Emphasize graphic clarity, route readability, and the transformation of people or vehicles into visual shapes. Maintain consistent overhead orientation throughout.

### Example

Use a strict top-down camera tracking above a courier running across a maze-like market rooftop route marked by colored tarps and narrow bridges. Emphasize route readability, repeating geometric shapes, and clear overhead orientation while the environment becomes a graphic map of movement.

Short add-on line: Use a strict top-down camera.

## Whip-Pan Reveal / Match Cut

A rapid pan reveals new information or hides a transition between two connected shots.

**Shot**: Any shot size, safest in Medium and Wide · **Lens**: 35mm, 50mm

### What changes

A rapid pan reveals new information or hides a transition between two connected shots.

### Use cases

Rhythm, comedy, action punctuation, hidden cut.

### Safer version

Match direction, blur density, and brightness if using it as a hidden cut.

### Risks

If the exit and entry blur do not match, the cut reads. Too much content during the blur creates noise.

### Prompt template

Execute a fast whip pan from [start subject/direction] to [new subject/information]. If used as a transition, let the frame enter full motion blur, then continue the same blur into the next shot before revealing the new scene. Keep the movement aggressive and readable.

### Example

Whip pan quickly from a chef shouting in a crowded kitchen to the dining room as a fire erupts near the bar. Let the frame enter full horizontal motion blur, then continue the same blur into the next view so the reveal lands as both a new piece of information and a seamless action accent.

Short add-on line: Execute a fast whip pan from [start subject/direction] to [new subject/information].

## Dolly Zoom

The subject remains approximately the same size while the space around them changes geometry.

Shot: Medium, Medium Close-Up, Full · Lens: 24mm to 70mm equivalent combination

### What changes

The subject remains approximately the same size while the space around them changes geometry.

### Use cases

Shock, alienation, realization, power shift.

### Safer version

Use one clean subject and a strong background with visible depth layers.

### Risks

Weak background depth makes the effect unclear. AI may replace the move with a simple zoom if the physical dolly is

### Prompt template

Maintain [subject] at approximately the same frame size while combining a physical dolly [in/out] with an opposing zoom [out/in]. Emphasize changing spatial geometry and preserved subject scale. End on a psychologically altered view of the space.

### Example

Keep a frightened bride at roughly the same medium-close framing while performing a physical dolly in combined with a zoom out. The cathedral behind her stretches and warps in perspective, creating a strong feeling of shock and disorientation as she realizes the groom never arrived.

Short add-on line: Maintain [subject] at approximately the same frame size while combining a physical dolly [in/out] with an opposing zoom [out/in].

## Camera Leaves The Hero

The camera begins with the hero but then abandons them to reveal consequences, threat, another character, or a more important space.

Shot: Medium, Wide, Tracking shots · Lens: 35mm, 50mm

### What changes

The camera begins with the hero but then abandons them to reveal consequences, threat, another character, or a more important space.

### Use cases

Consequences, dramatic irony, re-prioritization.

### Safer version

Make the second destination clearly more important than the hero.

### Risks

If the camera leaves without revealing something meaningful, the move feels arbitrary.

### Prompt template

Track with [hero] initially, then deliberately leave them and continue toward [consequence/threat/other character/key detail/independent space]. The move should clearly reassign narrative importance away from the hero.

### Example

Track with a boxer leaving the locker room toward the arena entrance, then let the camera abandon him and continue toward his young son watching nervously from the tunnel shadows. The move should clearly shift emotional importance away from the fighter and toward the child.

Short add-on line: Track with [hero] initially, then deliberately leave them and continue toward [consequence/threat/other character/key detail/independent space].

## Moving Master Through Several Beats

One continuous shot replaces several separate setups by carrying the scene through geography, dialogue, reaction, and reframe.

Shot: Wide to Medium to Close combinations · Lens: 28mm, 35mm, 40mm

### What changes

One continuous shot replaces several separate setups by carrying the scene through geography, dialogue, reaction, and reframe.

### Use cases

Scene coverage without cuts, performance continuity, blocking-led storytelling.

### Safer version

Build around three or four clear beats, not ten.

### Risks

Too many beats create confusion. Weak blocking makes the shot feel like wandering.

### Prompt template

Design one continuous moving master that covers several story beats without cutting: [wide geography], [interaction], [reaction], [closer emphasis]. Let camera movement and blocking reframe the scene naturally through each beat.

### Example

Design one continuous moving master inside a tense family dinner: begin wide to establish the table and all five relatives, move closer as the father questions the eldest son, drift into a two-shot for the mother's intervention, then land in a close-up as the son finally confesses the truth.

Short add-on line: Design one continuous moving master that covers several story beats without cutting: [wide geography], [interaction], [reaction], [closer emphasis].

## Foreground Occlusion Transition

A close foreground object fully covers the frame and reveals a new shot, time, or location.

Shot: Any size, safest in Medium and Wide · Lens: 35mm, 50mm

### What changes

A close foreground object fully covers the frame and reveals a new shot, time, or location.

### Use cases

Hidden transitions, continuity, AI consistency.

### Safer version

Use a full, readable occlusion that temporarily removes visual information.

### Risks

Partial occlusion often fails as a hidden cut. The cover object must dominate the frame.

### Prompt template

Let a close foreground object fully cover the lens, creating complete visual occlusion. Use that occlusion point to transition into [new scene/time/location], then clear the frame to reveal the next shot. Preserve directional continuity if needed.

### Example

Follow a woman through a busy train station until a dark coat passes extremely close to the lens and fully covers the frame. Use that total occlusion to transition into the same woman emerging years later from a subway exit in a different city, then clear the frame to reveal the new location.

Short add-on line: Let a close foreground object fully cover the lens, creating complete visual occlusion.

### Safer version

Limit the pass-off chain to two or three rigs for clarity.

### Risks

Too many pass-offs become confusing. The handoff moment must be motivated by proximity, occlusion, or path

## Reactive Handheld Snap Zoom

A reactive handheld camera responds to a sudden event with slight human delay, fast pan, snap zoom, brief blur, correction, and settle.

Shot: Medium, Wide, Action Medium Close-Up · Lens: 35mm, 50mm, zoom-capable doc style

### What changes

A reactive handheld camera responds to a sudden event with slight human delay, fast pan, snap zoom, brief blur, correction, and settle.

### Use cases

Documentary presence, chaos, adrenaline, grounding digital action.

### Safer version

Use one clean reaction chain: event, delay, pan, snap zoom, correction, settle.

### Risks

Continuous shaking ruins the effect. The human delay and corrective reframing are essential.

### Prompt template

Use a reactive handheld documentary camera inside [scene]. The operator follows [subject/event] with slight human delay, briefly loses perfect framing, then performs an abrupt snap zoom toward [target]. Include controlled handheld shake, rapid corrective reframing, brief motion smear during the zoom, subtle focus correction, heavy physical inertia, and a final settle instead of a weightless float.

### Example

Reactive handheld documentary camera inside a fully digital action scene. The operator follows the fast-moving creature with slight human delay, briefly loses perfect framing, then performs an abrupt snap zoom toward its face as it suddenly turns. Controlled handheld shake, rapid corrective reframing, brief motion smear during the zoom, subtle focus correction, heavy physical inertia. The camera settles after the reaction instead of floating smoothly.

Short add-on line: Use a reactive handheld documentary camera inside [scene].

## Ground-Level Vehicle Hard-Mount

The camera is mounted very low near the wheel, bumper, chassis, or suspension to emphasize speed, vibration, and danger.

Shot: Wide, Extreme low-angle mounted · Lens: 18mm, 24mm

### What changes

The camera is mounted very low near the wheel, bumper, chassis, or suspension to emphasize speed, vibration, and danger.

### Use cases

Velocity, mass, aggression, road contact.

**Safer version**

Stay close to one physical vehicle surface and let the world race by.

**Risks**

If the mount point is vague, the shot may float. Excessive distortion can look game-like if not grounded.

### Prompt template

Use a hard-mounted ground-level camera positioned near the [wheel/bumper/underside/suspension] of [vehicle]. Emphasize vibration, mass, speed, and dangerous proximity to the road surface. Keep the camera rigidly attached to the vehicle.

### Example

Use a hard-mounted ground-level camera near the front wheel and lower bumper of a rally car tearing across a wet mountain road. Emphasize rigid attachment, heavy vibration, water spray, dangerous proximity to the asphalt, and the violent speed of the passing terrain.

Short add-on line: Use a hard-mounted ground-level camera positioned near the [wheel/bumper/underside/suspension] of [vehicle].

### What changes

The action freezes or nearly freezes while the camera continues moving around a critical instant.

# Environment-Triggered Camera Reaction

The environment hits the camera after a force travels through space; the camera reacts only when the force physically reaches it.

Shot: Wide, Medium Wide, Documentary-style Medium · Lens: 24mm, 35mm, 50mm

### What changes

The environment hits the camera after a force travels through space; the camera reacts only when the force physically reaches it.

### Use cases

Explosions, giant steps, waves, collisions, turbulence.

### Safer version

Always stage the event first, then the traveling force, then the camera reaction.

### Risks

If the camera reacts before the force arrives, the physics feel fake.

### Prompt template

Stage [environmental event] first. Let the physical force travel through the environment, and only when it reaches the camera should the frame jolt, sway, or recover. Preserve cause and effect: event first, camera reaction second.

### Example

A giant creature slams its foot into the street several meters away. Dust, debris, and a visible shockwave travel toward the camera. Only when that force reaches the lens does the frame jolt sideways, tremble, and recover. Preserve strict physical cause and effect.

Short add-on line: Stage [environmental event] first.
