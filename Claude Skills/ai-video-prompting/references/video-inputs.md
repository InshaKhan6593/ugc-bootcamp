# Video inputs and settings

Text-to-video, image-to-video, start/end frames, omni-reference, focus and duration.

## AI Video Generation Settings, Explained Simply

## What you're building

Use this guide to understand the main AI video generation options in your image tool. AI video is not just writing one good prompt. It is about giving the model the right type of input for the result you want.

Sometimes the input is just text. Sometimes it is an image. Sometimes it is a start frame and end frame. Sometimes it is a character reference, product reference, audio file, voiceover, or full reference pack. The more specific the video needs to be, the more important those inputs become.

## 1. Text to video

Text to video is the simplest version of AI video. You write a prompt, press generate, and the model creates a video from your words alone. There is no image reference, no character reference, no first frame, no product photo, and no audio upload.

* Use text to video when you want to explore ideas quickly.

* Use it for concept testing.

* Use it when the exact character, product, or layout does not need to be perfect.

* Expect more variation because the model has more freedom.

The downside is control. Text to video can look amazing, but it may not match what you had in your head because the model is inventing everything from scratch.

## 2. Image to video

Image to video is where you upload an image and ask the model to animate it. Instead of asking the model to invent everything from text, you give it a visual starting point.

The image tells the model what the character looks like, what the product looks like, what the scene looks like, what the lighting is, what the camera angle is, and what the composition should be.

* Use image to video when you want more control.

* Use it for AI influencers, product ads, social pages, teaching material, cinematic scenes, and avatar videos.

* Get the image right first, then animate it.

* If the image is bad, the video will usually be bad too.

## 3. Start frames

A start frame is the first frame of your video. It tells the model exactly where the video begins and locks in the opening composition, subject, outfit, product, background, lighting, pose, angle, and visual style.

The beginner mistake is re-describing the whole image inside the video prompt. You do not need to say everything that is already visible. The video model can see the image. Your prompt should focus on what should happen.

Start frame example

`Start frame: coffee cup on a wooden table.`
`Video prompt: steam gently rises from the coffee while the camera slowly pushes in.`

## 4. End frames

An end frame is the final frame you want the video to move towards. When you combine a start frame and an end frame, you give the model both a starting point and an end destination.

* Use end frames for transitions.

* Use them for transformations and before/after shots.

* Use them for product reveals.

* Use them for character movement, scene changes, and controlled camera moves.

Your start and end frames should feel connected. If they are too different, the model may morph strangely, lose consistency, or create weird movement. The best end frames feel like a natural continuation of the first frame.

## 5. Omni-reference

Omni-reference means giving the model multiple references at once. Instead of relying only on a text prompt, start frame, or end frame, you give the model more context.

* Character reference: what the person should look like.

* Product reference: what the product should look like.

* Location reference: what the setting should feel like.

* Style reference: the realism, lighting, or visual treatment.

* Pose or movement reference: how the body or camera should behave.

* Audio reference or voiceover: what the scene should be built around.

You can toggle Omni-reference on models like Seedance. The more complex the scene is, the more useful a reference pack becomes.

## 6. Example: building a UGC reference pack

If you want a UGC ad where a woman holds a supplement bottle in a bright kitchen and speaks to camera with natural phone realism, references help the model understand the job.

* Upload the product image so the bottle stays accurate.

* Upload a character reference so the person stays consistent.

* Upload a location or style reference for kitchen realism and lighting.

* Upload audio or a voiceover so the scene is built around what the person says.

UGC voiceover example

`Guys, I have to tell you about this supplement I have been taking. I was skeptical at first, but I have been taking these for a month and I actually feel a difference.`

## 7. Camera movement

Camera movement is one of the biggest things that separates a boring AI video from a stronger one. You need to think about what the camera is doing, not just what is in the scene.

* Is the camera slowly pushing in?

* Is it pulling back?

* Is it orbiting around the subject?

* Is it handheld?

* Is it static on a tripod?

* Is it following the person?

* Is it close-up, wide, or moving left to right?

The Assist mode on the prompt bar can help with different camera movement ideas. The camera angle PDFs in the course can also be used for inspiration.

## 8. Keep motion focused

AI video models can struggle when you ask for too much at once. If the character is running, jumping, spinning, changing outfit, picking up a product, talking to camera, and the camera is flying around the room, you are giving the model too many chances to break.

* One main subject movement.

* One main camera movement.

* One clear scene.

* That is usually enough.

Seedance is strong with movement, but even strong models can glitch or morph if the prompt is overcrowded. Clean motion usually beats complicated motion.

## 9. Duration and shot-based thinking

Most AI video clips are still short. Many models are designed for short clips rather than full long-form scenes. That means you need to think in individual shots and edit them together.

* Do not try to make a long advert or story in one prompt.

* Break the video into scenes.

* Generate each shot with a clear purpose.

* Edit the shots together afterwards.

The big idea: better input creates better output. Do not just write a prompt and hope. Give the model a strong image input, clear references, and a focused motion prompt.
