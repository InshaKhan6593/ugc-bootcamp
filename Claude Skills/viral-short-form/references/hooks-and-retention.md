# Hooks and retention

Every second of a short-form video has one job: buy the next second. The hook buys the first three; the structure buys the rest. Watch time is the signal the platform cares about most, so this is where reach is actually decided.

## The three-second rule

Make the first clip no longer than about three seconds. If the viewer has to wait five seconds for a scene change, they will scroll - not because the content is bad, but because nothing has happened yet.

Three consequences:

1. **Open mid-action.** No logo, no intro, no "hey guys", no establishing shot. Start where the interesting part starts.
2. **Front-load the promise.** The viewer should know within a second what they are going to get.
3. **Change something fast.** A cut, a reveal, a movement, text appearing - the screen must not be static while the hook is spoken.

## Hook patterns

| Type | Shape | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Curiosity gap | State an outcome, withhold the mechanism | "This took me four hours and nobody believes it's AI." |
| Contrarian | Deny the thing the audience believes | "Everyone's doing this backwards." |
| Confession | Admit a mistake or a cost | "I wasted two years on this." |
| Pattern interrupt | Say something that does not fit the format | "This is going to sound fake... but it isn't." |
| Insider | Imply privileged knowledge | "People don't realise this yet." |
| Stakes | Name what goes wrong | "No one warned me about this." |
| Visual hook | The image is the hook; the words are secondary | A 300-foot octopus on a ship's deck |
| Question | Ask something the viewer answers internally | "Why does every AI video look the same?" |
| Number | A specific, slightly odd figure | "Eleven generations to get one usable shot." |
| List promise | Name the payoff count | "Three things I'd never do again." |
| POV | Place the viewer in the scene | "POV: you're the first one off the landing craft." |
| Negative | Tell them not to do something | "Stop using 'cinematic lighting' in your prompts." |

For visual niches the hook is the frame itself, so the first image has to be the most arresting one you have. Do not save the best shot for the middle.

## Writing a hook

Write the hook before the video, not after. If you cannot write a hook for an idea, the idea is weak - and it is far cheaper to discover that now.

Then test it against four questions:

1. Would this stop *me* scrolling?
2. Is it specific? ("I made £4k" beats "I made money".)
3. Does it work muted, as on-screen text?
4. Can it be said in two seconds?

Write ten and pick one. The tenth is usually better than the first, because the first is the obvious one everyone writes.

For a clone or talking-head, keep the hook short and easy to say - a hook that needs four seconds of setup gives the viewer time to judge the face before the content lands (`ai-clone-yourself/references/clone-scripts.md`).

## Retention structures

### The short loop (7-15 seconds)
Hook - escalation - payoff - and the last frame flows into the first, so it loops invisibly. Loops inflate watch time per view more than any other device.

### The promise and delivery (15-30 seconds)
Hook states a promise - one obstacle or complication - delivery - a single-line CTA. The complication is what stops it feeling like an ad.

### The list (20-45 seconds)
Hook names the count - items in descending order of obviousness, so the best is last - payoff. Saying "the third one is the one nobody does" at the start buys the whole middle.

### The story (30-60 seconds)
Situation - the thing that went wrong - what you did - result - what it means for the viewer. Dialogue and specifics carry it; summaries kill it.

### The demo (15-30 seconds)
Problem shown, not described - the method - the result on screen. For products, the demonstration *is* the proof, which means no claims are needed.

### The reaction or reveal (10-20 seconds)
Setup that implies one outcome - the actual outcome. The whole video is the gap between the two.

## Keeping attention mid-video

The middle is where most videos lose people, and the cause is nearly always that nothing changed for too long.

- **Change something every two to three seconds** - a cut, a camera move, text, a new shot size, a sound.
- **Vary shot size.** Wide, medium, close, detail. Identical framing for twenty seconds reads as flat regardless of content.
- **Cut away over any weak moment** rather than regenerating it.
- **Trim every pause.** Short-form has no tolerance for breathing room that is not deliberate.
- **Escalate.** Each beat should be bigger, stranger or more specific than the last.
- **Use sound as structure.** A sound effect on a cut, a music change at the turn, silence before the payoff.
- **Keep text moving.** Captions that change hold the eye; a static text block gets ignored after two seconds.

## Rewatch devices

A rewatch is worth more than a view, and these produce them cheaply:

- **Invisible loops** - the last frame matches the first.
- **A detail that only makes sense afterwards** - something in the background the payoff explains.
- **Fast information** - a number, a name or a list that goes by too quickly to absorb once.
- **A question at the end** that sends people back to check.
- **A deliberately odd detail** people want to look at again. This doubles as comment bait (`engagement-tactics.md`).

## Testing hooks

Change one variable at a time, or the result tells you nothing:

1. Same video, three different hooks, posted on different days.
2. Same hook, two different first frames.
3. Same content, text hook versus spoken hook.

Read the two-second retention number, not the view count - views are downstream of distribution, which is downstream of retention. Then double down on the hook style that wins, and keep reusing it until it stops working.

For clone and ad content, the hook can be regenerated alone and spliced onto an existing body, which makes hook testing nearly free (`ai-clone-yourself/references/editing-and-broll.md`).
