## STEP 1: BUILD THE CAST CHARACTER PASSPORTS

Before a single frame exists, every character is locked into one image, the character sheet. That image is the passport: the same person from every angle, in the same clothes, under the same light. From then on the passport is the character. It goes into every shot as a reference, and the model is left with nothing to invent.

| ------------- | ------ | ---------- |
| GPT Image 2.0 | 16:9   | 9          |

### BASIC PASSPORT

USE THIS PROMPT

Photorealistic character reference sheet: the SAME person repeated in multiple views across one seamless off-white studio background, arranged as a clean horizontal grid. Editorial lookbook photography, 85mm lens, soft even diffused frontal studio light, gentle contact shadow under the feet, no harsh shadows, neutral colour, ultra-realistic skin texture with visible pores and natural imperfections, razor-sharp focus, high-end fashion catalogue quality.

LAYOUT, left to right - every panel shows the same person in the same outfit:

1. Full-body FRONT view. Head to shoes fully inside the frame. Standing straight, arms relaxed at the sides, neutral expression, facing camera.
2. Full-body BACK view. Identical pose, showing the back of the garment.
3. Large MEDIUM shot, chest-up, three-quarter angle, natural relaxed posture.
4. Narrow right column of three stacked head-and-shoulders portraits: side profile, three-quarter angle, and frontal.

CHARACTER - [age, build, ethnicity, hair, facial hair, skin, tattoos, scars, expression]

WARDROBE - [every garment, colour, material, logo and its exact placement, footwear, jewellery, headwear, eyewear]

Identical clothing, hair, jewellery and body proportions in every panel. Identical lighting in every panel. No text, no labels, no watermarks, no visible borders between panels.

Avoid: illustration, 3D render, plastic or waxy skin, airbrushed retouching, outfit changing between panels, cropped feet, duplicated limbs.

FILL TWO SLOTS

CHARACTER

Age, build, height. Ethnicity. Hair colour, length and how it sits. Facial hair. Skin tone and texture. Tattoos and exactly where they fall on the body. Scars, piercings, teeth. Default expression.

### WARDROBE

Every garment by name, colour and material. Logos and their exact placement: chest, back, sleeve. Footwear. Jewellery. Headwear. Eyewear. When the shape of a garment matters, describe the shape, not the brand.

THEN LOCK DOWN

### WARDROBE

Same garments, same colours, same logo placement in every shot of the film.

### HAIR

Same length, same parting, same styling or tie-back.

### SKIN

Tattoos in the same places, at the same size, in the same colours.

### JEWELLERY

Chains, rings, earrings. Either present in every shot or in none.

### EYEWEAR AND HEADWEAR

Decide once whether the character wears it. On in one shot and off in the next breaks the film faster than anything else.

### PROPORTIONS

Build and height never change. Left alone, the model makes everyone slimmer and taller.

## STEP 2: LOCATIONS

The look of the film is decided here. Not in the character shots and not in the grade, but in the location: film stock, hour of day and sun direction are set once. That is why this section opens with the look block, copied into every location without a single edit and carried on into the shot prompts, every time. A location is a place, not a picture. The moment the camera moves, the model invents everything again unless you tell it where it now stands relative to the last view.

## VISUAL DNA

COPIED INTO EVERY LOCATION AND EVERY SHOT · NEVER EDITED

THE LOOK - paste this block into every location prompt and every shot prompt, word for word, never edited.

Shot on 35mm film, Kodak Vision3 500T, tungsten-balanced stock exposed in daylight through a warm 85 correction. Late golden hour, roughly forty minutes before sunset, the sun low at about ten degrees above the horizon. Warm golden-peach light on every surface facing the sun, cool pale blue-white sky, long soft shadows raking across the ground. Gentle halation blooming around the brightest highlights, fine organic grain, soft highlight roll-off, deep shadows that never crush to black. Natural colour, the way a real film stock renders a real street.

No teal-and-orange grading. No HDR. No digital clarity or sharpening. No lens flare. No dramatic clouds. Nothing wet unless it rained in the story.

No teal-and-orange grading. No HDR. No digital clarity or sharpening. No lens flare. No dramatic clouds.

\=== PLACE - changes for every location ===

Photorealistic establishing plate of [WHAT THE PLACE IS]. [LENS]mm spherical lens, camera at standing eye level, perfectly level horizon, no tilt, no wide-angle distortion. The sun rakes in from [DIRECTION]. Documentary stillness, nobody in frame.

Foreground - [what is closest to the camera: the road surface, the kerb, the grass, the dust]

Midground - [the buildings, their material and colour, the vehicles, the fences]
Background - [what closes the frame at the far end]

Real-world imperfection: cracked asphalt, dry yellow grass, sun-faded paint, overhead

power lines on wooden poles.

This must read as a photograph of a real street, not as a render.

Avoid: people, tilted horizon, oversaturation, staged tidiness.

## CHANGING THE LOCATION VIEWPOINT

FOR THE MAIN LOCATIONS · THE CAMERA DOES NOT MOVE, IT ONLY ROTATES

Same location as the reference image. The camera has not moved from its position, it has rotated [DEGREES] degrees to the [LEFT / RIGHT] and now faces [WHAT IS THERE].

KEEP IDENTICAL - the entire LOOK block, the sun in the same place in the sky, shadows falling in the same direction and at the same length, the same ground surface continuing underfoot, the same horizon height, the same lens, the same eye level.

WHAT STAYS VISIBLE - [the piece of the previous frame that must still be in view at the edge, so the two frames physically connect]

WHAT IS NEW - [what the rotation reveals]

Nobody in frame. This is one continuous place photographed from one standing position, not two different places.

## ANOTHER VIEW OF THE SAME PLACE

THE MASTER PLATE GOES IN AS REFERENCE · THE CAMERA CHANGES POSITION

The same location as in the reference image, photographed from a different camera position.

CAMERA - [where the camera now stands and which way it faces. For example: the camera has turned 180 degrees and now looks back out of the cul-de-sac towards the main road.]

KEEP IDENTICAL - the entire LOOK block, time of day, height and direction of the sun, colour and temperature of the light, length and direction of the shadows, architecture and its materials, road surface, vegetation, sky, film stock and grain. Same lens, same standing eye level, same level horizon.

NEW IN FRAME - [what becomes visible from this position that was behind the camera before]

Nobody in frame. Same documentary stillness. This is the same place at the same minute, only the camera has moved.

### FILL THREE SLOTS

#### WHERE THE CAMERA STANDS

Not "a view of the street" but a camera position: deep in the cul-de-sac, at the kerb, in the middle of the road. The model puts the camera exactly where your words put it.

#### WHICH WAY IT FACES

Direction matters more than content. If the camera turned 180 degrees, write that.

### THE SUN

Height and direction. If shadows fall to the right in one frame, they fall to the right in all of them.

### TIME OF DAY

One hour for the whole location. Golden hour does not last forty minutes in life, but it does in a film.

### CAMERA HEIGHT

Eye level by default. Change it only when it is a decision, not an accident.

### LENS

One focal length per location. A different focal length reads as a different place.

### MATERIALS

Stucco, asphalt, chain-link, dry grass. List them once and repeat them verbatim.

### SKY

Colour and condition. Clouds appearing break a location harder than a moved building.

### EMPTINESS

Not one accidental person. People appear only where the story puts them.

Guys, think like a cinematographer on real film-set rather than someone ordering a picture. A cinematographer never says "show me the street"; he says "camera is here, facing that way, the exit is behind us".

## VEHICLES AND PROPS OBJECT PASSPORTS

You need to protect the objects. A viewer will not notice that the bat got longer, but they will stop believing the shot. If you are putting an object into more than one scene, you need an object passport.

### OBJECT PASSPORT

Photorealistic object reference sheet on one seamless pale grey studio background, arranged as a clean two-row grid. Product photography, 85mm lens, soft even diffused studio light from above and slightly front, gentle contact shadow under the object, no harsh reflections, neutral colour, razor-sharp focus, high-end catalogue quality.

TOP ROW - six orthographic views of the same object, evenly spaced, identical scale, each with a small uppercase sans-serif label beneath:
FRONT / FRONT 3-4 LEFT / LEFT SIDE / RIGHT SIDE / REAR 3-4 RIGHT / REAR

BOTTOM ROW - five macro detail panels, each labelled, showing the parts that make this object recognisable: [name the five details that matter for your object, for example logo, seam, hardware, texture, worn edge]

OBJECT - [what it is, its exact material and finish, colour, scale relative to a human, condition and wear, any marking or logo and its exact placement]

Identical object, identical finish and identical lighting in every panel. Nothing else in frame. No people, no props, no environment, no background scenery.

Avoid: dramatic rim light, coloured gels, reflections of a studio, floating shadowless object, plastic CGI look.

## OBJECT STATE

THE BASE PASSPORT GOES IN AS REFERENCE · ONLY THE STATE CHANGES

The same object as in the reference sheet, in a different state.

KEEP IDENTICAL - the sheet layout, the seamless pale grey background, the soft even light, the contact shadow, the camera angles, the scale, the position and style of the labels.

WHAT CHANGES - [the new state: new paint, new material, new configuration, new fittings]

WHAT MUST NOT CHANGE - [the geometry, proportions and identifying details that prove it is still the same object]

Both sheets must be able to sit side by side and read as one object photographed twice, not as two objects.

FILL THREE SLOTS

THE OBJECT

What it is, what it is made of, its size relative to a person, its condition. Wear and use matter more than shine: a brand-new object always looks like a render.

FIVE DETAILS

Name the five macro panels your object needs. On a bike: bars, weld, crank, wheel, saddle. On a ring: face, setting, shoulder, inner band. The model will not guess what matters to you.

MARKINGS

Logo, number, lettering and exactly where they sit. This is what lets a viewer recognise the object in a shot faster than its shape does.

LOCK ACROSS ALL OBJECTS

BACKGROUND AND LIGHT

The same seamless pale grey and the same soft light on every object in the film. A different background turns a set of passports into a set of borrowed pictures.

SCALE

An object has to look at its real size. The ring and the Impala are shot the same way, but they must never end up the same height in frame.
